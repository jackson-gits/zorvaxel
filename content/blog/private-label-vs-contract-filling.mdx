Choosing how to make a product is an early decision with lasting effects. The terms *private label* and *contract filling* are sometimes used loosely, so ask what a supplier actually provides rather than relying on the name. In general, private label means choosing a product the supplier already makes and selling it under your brand. Contract filling means arranging for a supplier to fill a product to an agreed specification.

## What is the difference?

With private label, you usually select from a supplier’s existing formulas or products. You add your brand, choose from the available packaging options, and order according to the supplier’s requirements. The formula and production process are already in place, though the supplier may limit changes.

With contract filling, you bring a ready serum or a defined brief to a manufacturer that can fill and pack it. The formula might be yours, supplied by a partner, or developed with the manufacturer. “Filling” does not automatically mean the supplier will formulate the product, source every ingredient, or design the packaging. Confirm the exact scope before comparing offers.

## Who supplies the formula and packaging?

A private-label supplier typically provides its existing formula and production setup. You may choose among standard bottles, closures, labels or sizes. You usually provide brand assets and approve artwork. Ask whether the product can be customised, whether other brands can use the same formula, and who owns any changes.

For contract filling, you and the manufacturer decide who supplies each part. You might provide finished bulk serum and empty bottles, or ask the manufacturer to source them. The manufacturer must confirm that the formula, container and filling equipment are compatible. If the formula or package is new, allow time for samples, checks and approval before production. Put responsibility for artwork, packaging specifications, testing and quality checks in writing.

  Under conventional private-label contracts, the manufacturer retains 100% of the formulation IP. If your brand scales and you need to switch suppliers or expand capacity, you cannot take the exact recipe with you. With contract filling and custom development, ensure your agreement explicitly guarantees brand ownership of the Master Formula Record.

## Which route fits your first product?

Private label can suit a founder who wants to test demand with an existing formula and does not need a distinctive formula straight away. It can reduce development decisions, but it may limit ingredient, texture and packaging choices. Another brand could choose a similar base product.

Contract filling may suit a business that already has a formula or needs a defined product specification. It can offer more control over the brief, but brings coordination: the formula, ingredients, packaging and equipment must all work together. Neither option guarantees a faster launch, lower total cost or better margins. The right choice depends on your product and the written scope.

## Compare the full scope, not the label

Common cost drivers include formula development or adaptation, ingredients, packaging, labels, testing, setup, order quantity, storage and freight. A per-unit price may omit one-time charges or packaging minimums. Ask each supplier to price the same product, fill volume, package and quantity. Have the quote separate one-time work from repeat production charges and list what is not included.

Before paying, request the minimum order, sample process, payment milestones and expected timeline. Confirm who owns the formula, artwork and tooling, whether a sample is representative of production, and how defects or changes are handled. Ask what you need to provide and when. Get responsibilities and cancellation terms in writing.

## A simple decision guide

If an existing formula and standard package meet your needs, ask about private label. If you have a ready serum and need bottles filled, ask about contract filling. If you need a new formula or a specific package, first confirm that the supplier offers that work and what development, testing and minimums apply.

Choose the model whose written scope, ownership terms and total commitments fit your product. The service name matters less than knowing exactly who is doing what before the first batch begins.
