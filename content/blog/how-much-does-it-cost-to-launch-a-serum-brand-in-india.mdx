There is no single price to launch a serum brand. A manufacturing quote covers only the work named in it. Your full launch budget may also include formula development, packaging, samples, testing, artwork, shipping and the cost of getting the product ready to sell. Start by separating those lines rather than comparing one headline unit price.

## A published example, not a standard rate

One Indian skincare manufacturer’s guide, published in August 2025, lists facial serum at **₹200–₹300 per unit for a 300-unit order**. The guide says its estimate includes formulation, manufacturing and basic packaging. At that stated rate, the product-only arithmetic for 300 units is **₹60,000–₹90,000**. The page does not specify a bottle size for that line, so it may not match your product. These are the supplier’s indicative figures, not a market survey or a current quote for your product. Ask for a written estimate based on your actual formula, fill size and pack.

The same guide lists **₹5,000–₹20,000 per product for research and development** and **₹10,000–₹30,000 for trials and testing**, depending on complexity. Treat these as separate examples from that one supplier. Ask whether a proposed charge covers sample iterations, testing methods, reports, or only initial work. A different Indian serum manufacturer describes its minimum order options as flexible but publishes no unit prices, another reason to request a quote against your own brief.

  A ₹250 per-bottle headline price is not a quote until you know whether the dropper assembly, outer carton, label printing, and stability testing are included. Separating one-time R&D fees from repeat per-unit production costs is the only way to avoid mid-launch cash crunches.

## What the first quote may leave out

“Per bottle” can mean different things. One price may include the formula, filling and a standard bottle. Another may exclude the dropper, label, carton, testing, artwork or delivery. Request an itemised quote that says exactly what is included, what is charged once, and what repeats with each order. Check whether taxes, sample dispatch, custom printing and wastage allowances are listed separately.

A new formula also changes the budget. You may need time for samples and compatibility checks before approving a production batch. Ask what happens if a sample needs adjustment, who supplies ingredients and packaging, and whether you can approve the final filled pack before the run begins. Do not assume a test or certificate is included because the words appear in a sales conversation; have the scope stated in writing.

## Why a small batch is not a smaller large-order quote

A 50-bottle first run is not automatically one-sixth of a 300-bottle price. Setup, sourcing and packaging minimums can be charged differently from the liquid inside each bottle. Standard packaging may keep choices simpler; custom colours, printing or cartons may introduce separate minimums. Ask the manufacturer to compare two real quantities using the same formula and the same package, and to show which one-time costs are shared across the order.

## Make a budget you can compare

Write down your formula and fill size, bottle and closure, label or carton, order quantity, sample and testing needs, and delivery destination. Then ask each manufacturer to price the same specification. Keep one-time development charges separate from the repeatable per-unit cost. Add a contingency only after you know what is excluded; avoid copying an online total built for a different product or order size.

The most useful first budget is not the lowest estimate. It is the one that lets you see what you are paying for, what can change the total, and what decision comes next. Start with a batch you can afford to test, get the scope in writing, and revise the plan when you have a firm quote.
